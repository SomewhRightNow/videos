This summer, Norway turned the World Cup into a Viking ship.
This is the story of the Viking Row. Where it came from, and why the whole world started rowing.
To understand it, you have to go back more than a thousand years.
To an age when ships like these ruled the northern seas.
In nineteen oh four, archaeologists dug one out of a burial mound in southern Norway. The Oseberg ship.
It had been buried in the year eight thirty-four, and it had survived.
Its cousin, the Gokstad ship, had thirty-two oars. Sixteen on each side.
And every oar had to hit the water at the same moment.
So the crew rowed to a rhythm. One beat. One call. Pull together, or go nowhere.
Today, replicas still sail these waters, and crews still pull the oars the old way.
The Draken Harald Horfagreh, the biggest Viking ship built in modern times, even crossed the Atlantic.
Now, fast forward to Norwegian football.
For years, Norway was the team that never quite made it. No World Cup since nineteen ninety-eight.
Then came a new generation. Haaland. Oedegaard. And a superfan named Ole Froy-stad.
Fans know him as Mister Row-Row.
Months before the World Cup, at a Norway match, he tried something new.
Sit down on the stand. Shoulder to shoulder. Like rowers on a longship.
A drum sets the beat. Everyone pulls an invisible oar. And on every stroke, one word. Roh! Norwegian for row.
It looked ridiculous. It sounded incredible. And it spread.
If this reminds you of something, you're not wrong.
In twenty sixteen, Iceland shook Europe with the Viking Thunder Clap. One slow, giant clap echoing around France.
But Iceland's clap was a war cry. Norway's row is a voyage.
Summer twenty twenty-six. Norway are back at the World Cup for the first time in twenty-eight years.
And their fans bring the ship with them.
After the win over Senegal, the players joined the row with their fans.
The videos exploded. The row reached Times Square. Even Google gave it its own animation.
Back home, thousands rowed together in the streets of Oslo.
Then came Brazil. Haaland scored twice. Norway won two-one and reached their first ever World Cup quarter-final.
The journey ended against England, after extra time.
But nobody remembers that summer for the scoreline.
They remember the sound. Thousands of people. One drum. All pulling in the same direction.
Because that is what a Viking crew was. And that is what a team is.
